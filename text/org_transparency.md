Here, we explore the general distribution and variation of the transparency scores, with a more granular breakdown by organization types. 

Industry and Academia developers are the most prolific in the dataset, and both show broad variability in transparency. Models from these groups span the entire scale—from fully transparent (score of 4) to completely opaque (score of 0)—indicating that transparency is not consistent within these sectors. This suggests the presence of both leaders and laggards in disclosure practices. 

Academic institutions, seen as champions of open research, surprisingly reveal transparency patterns comparable to Industry developers. While some academic developers disclosed full training metadata, others omitted key components—reflecting shifting incentives in academia, including competition for funding and publication speed, which may be diminishing the emphasis on thorough documentation (Chakravoti & Koneru, 2024).

In contrast, research collectives and cross-sector collaborations stand out for their higher median transparency scores (3/4.0) and more consistent practices, with fewer low-scoring models. Organizations like EleutherAI and the Allen Institute for AI, which prioritize open science practices and reproducibility (Phang et al., 2022; Allen Institute for AI, 2025), achieved high transparency scores of 4.0 and 3.0, respectively. While these groups represent a smaller share of the dataset, their models are more likely to report key training inputs, potentially reflecting stronger norms or shared commitments to openness.

Government and public sector developers fall somewhere in the middle, with a moderate range of transparency and more variability than collaborative efforts. Differences likely reflect national rather than sector-specific constraints. For example, the Technology Innovation Institute in the UAE achieved a perfect score (4.0), while the National Cancer Institute (USA) and Alan Turing Institute (UK) scored substantially lower (2.0 and 1.0, respectively). However, given the small sample size of public sector models, these comparisons should be interpreted with caution.

The “Unknown” category is small but scattered across the scale, hinting at occasional gaps in organizational metadata itself.

Together, these patterns show that no organization type guarantees transparency, but collaborative and research-driven entities tend to be more consistent and higher-scoring overall. This points to the importance of institutional norms and incentives in shaping disclosure practices, not just sector. Efforts to improve disclosure should therefore go beyond technical reporting templates and address the underlying drivers of behaviour. Integrating transparency expectations into funding, publishing, and procurement processes, as well as drawing on high-performing collaborative models as benchmarks, could help raise standards across all developer types.
